What would an AI-native organization learn?
Will Brown asks what it would take for agents to learn how an organization actually works. A startup using coding agents everywhere is one version of an AI-native company. Integrating agents throughout an established organization raises a different question: can the organization’s accumulated experience make its agents better over time?
Prime Intellect builds infrastructure for training, evaluating, deploying, and improving models, especially open models. Brown positions this work alongside frontier models. The opportunity is to adapt models to a company’s own workflows and harnesses, then let their capabilities grow with its experience.
A company has an org chart, and then it has the actual routes by which decisions get made. Experienced employees learn who can unblock a project, which process causes friction, and which team has already solved a recurring problem. Much of that knowledge never becomes a rule in a document.
Agents can already read across emails, code, and process documents to find repeated problems. A team may be spending time on something another team has solved. An agent with access to both can connect those observations. Brown wants that discovery to feed a continuing improvement process, rather than become a report the company produces once.

Decide what belongs in context and what merits training
Today’s agents often learn through their current context. Someone writes a skill file, the agent explores a repository, or a user corrects a mistake. Much of the next run then involves rediscovering information or loading the same instructions again.
A cache can lower the price of repeated input, but it does not eliminate the need to give the model the relevant material. Brown distinguishes explicit rules from patterns that are harder to write down. Preferences, recurring friction, and situational judgment may be expressed more naturally through examples than through an ever-growing instruction file.
That makes training attractive, but he cautions that information has to earn its place in the weights. Skills, memory, retrieval, and model training remain different tools for different kinds of knowledge.
To explain the distance between domain expertise and model training, Brown traces a hypothetical route. Someone with experience using a business tool supplies knowledge through an expert network. Other people turn that into a reinforcement-learning environment. A lab incorporates it into a training run. Each stage can be useful, yet the expert who knows the job may never supervise the resulting model directly.
An employee correcting a coding agent has a shorter path available: in this situation, the agent did one thing, and the employee wanted another. That is contextual preference data. Brown’s proposed learning systems would turn those corrections into model improvements instead of leaving every lesson as another line in
AGENTS.md
.
The aim is to preserve the judgment behind the correction. A rule can say what to do in a known case; examples can help a model learn which response fits related cases it has not seen before.

Begin with test cases, then learn the routing decision
Training on organizational data is too broad a starting brief. Brown recommends beginning with evaluations: treat a model as something with a development lifecycle, much as a codebase accumulates tests and changes over time.
The raw material is already appearing in agent traces. They contain the user’s task, what the model did, and feedback on the result. Curation turns those traces into repeatable cases. Agents can help with that work, while people supply the judgments that make a case useful.
Routing provides a bounded first target. Run a large model and a smaller model on the same task. If evaluation shows that the smaller model’s result is good enough, that comparison becomes evidence for routing similar work to it. A decision model in the harness can learn the choice among a frontier model, a cheaper open model, and a model trained for the organization.

Ask for decisions in a form the model can learn from
More agents create a coordination problem for their human users. A person may start with one thread, then add another until there are twenty places to check for questions. Combining everything into one stream makes the questions harder to find in a different way.
Brown wants agents to run with an idea and return when a decision is needed. He has been experimenting with interfaces generated for the particular choice, rather than asking people to search a long conversation for the relevant question.
The example decision card asks whether to promote a model that improved on four evaluation suites but slowed down on handwritten forms. The user can promote it, hold it, or inspect cases.
The interface example at
7:21:35
gives the user a specific decision and the evidence relevant to it. The surrounding list holds other pending work.
This improves two interactions at once. The person can answer a focused question without reconstructing the whole thread. The training system receives a choice tied to the situation that produced it. That is a clearer target than inferring a preference from a long, ambiguous exchange.
Brown presents this as an experimental starting point for learning from human input. The approach can train an orchestration or decision model without requiring every model in the stack to be retrained. It also gives the team an explicit target to inspect when considering problems such as reward hacking.

Make improvement a recurring job
Brown points to Paper Instruments as an example of the scale becoming accessible. The slide names its
Ultramarine 1 Flash
knowledge-work model and reports a five-person team and about $300,000 in training cost. He compares that spending to an employee-scale investment. It is an example he cites, not a general budget for training any specialized model.
The larger proposal is to spread learning over time. During the day, collect traces and feedback while agents do the organization’s work. At night, review a proposed experiment, inspect the cases that exposed problems, and start a training run. Compute used for inference during working hours might be available for training when demand falls.
The internal prototype he shows keeps evaluation history alongside that process.
At
7:24:05
, the slide labels the interface an internal prototype and describes scoring each checkpoint on every suite it has seen. It presents a way to inspect ongoing learning, not a claim that the whole workflow is already generally available.
Brown’s sequence starts well before the first nightly training job. Discover the workflow. Create useful skills. Build evaluations. Learn where routing helps. Only then does the accumulated evidence support investing in models that retain more of the organization’s knowledge.
The company gets better agents by preserving the lessons from work it is already doing. For Brown, that is how continual learning becomes an organizational practice: a lifecycle of observed failures, deliberate changes, and evaluations that make the next model’s behavior inspectable.
